The first drawing usually comes before the first rule check.

Whether a non-profit housing scheme in British Columbia is allowed, and whether a funder would back it, is written across zoning, provincial law, City policy, design standards and funding calls. Each has its own owner and gets read at its own time, usually after design has started. Funding-Fit Memo checks a site and a building option against all of them on day one, and shows its working.

The rules are public and dated. They sit in five layers, read by different people at different times.

A scheme on church land in Vancouver answers to its zoning district, to provincial overrides, to the City's rezoning policies, to design standards and to the funding programs themselves. The tool's rule stack holds 17 rules from 13 public sources. On a small project nobody reads all of them before the first sketch, and nobody writes down which version they read.

Three answers, each with its working shown.

Give it a site file and an option file, a page of JSON each. It returns a memo that answers three questions in order, and a ledger with one row per rule: the numbers it used, the clause it came from, the version in force on the date, and who resolves anything left open.

When a rule changes, the memo shows it and the stamp changes.

Every rule carries dated versions. The engine applies the ones in force on the as-of date and stamps the memo with a hash of them, so two memos can be told apart and an old one can be reproduced. The City's family-unit rule gets a new version on 27 Oct 2026. Here is the same option, read on either side of that date.

What changed nothing yet: read as of 23 Oct, the memo uses the 1992 version. The new version takes effect on 27 Oct 2026; switch the date to see the revision. Revision A 1 rule, cov.family_units: version 1992-01-01 → 2026-10-27, pass → pass, stamp 20e44f1445ecaff9 → 6d1b07fe59dd3775.

Same verdict, new version: the memo names the version it used, and the new stamp tells the two memos apart.

Same inputs and the same rules give the same ledger, byte for byte. No language model runs inside the engine: it is standard-library Python and plain JSON rule files a firm can read and maintain, and a rule change is recorded as a new dated version, so the old memo can still be reproduced.

What it does not do

  • It does not decide. Every conditional and uncertain finding is handed to a named person.
  • It does not read maps or PDFs. The rule files were transcribed by hand from the cited documents, with notes where a figure should be re-checked. The transit-oriented area tier is read from the City's map by a person.
  • It is a screen, not advice. Nothing in the memo is legal, planning or funding advice; every number links to the document it came from, so check it there.
  • Its demo has declared placeholders. The red pine's diameter and the setbacks are assumed, the rent tiers and accessible units are left blank, and the what-if option is hypothetical and says so. The other inputs come from the City's referral report.

See it work on a real case.

998 E 19th Ave, Vancouver: six storeys of social housing over a rebuilt church, approved by Council on 25 Jul 2024. Its inputs come from the City's public referral report, apart from the declared placeholders; the rules come from the by-laws, policies and funding documents they cite.
