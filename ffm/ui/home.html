01The problem

Today the first reliable answer costs six figures.

Future of Good and WC Studio put a project's pre‑development at $200,000 to $500,000, and a board votes that money on a feasibility read that cites no sources and carries no date. First Read puts a dated, cited answer in front of the board first: gathering the inputs takes a few hours, and the run takes under a second.

02What it found

Four answers on one sheet.

This is the memo for 998 E 19th Ave, six storeys of social housing on church land that Council approved in July 2024. Its inputs come from the City's public referral report.

Site998 E 19th Ave, Vancouver · RM-1
OptionAs approved · 6 storeys · 105 units
RevisionR1 · rules as of 23 Oct 2026
Rezoning pathCD-1 under the Interim Rezoning Policy

1 · Is it allowed?

Not outright. By rezoning: approved.

A floor space ratio (FSR) of 2.95 against 1.20 allowed outright, and 22.3 m tall against 10.7 m. Council approved the rezoning on 25 July 2024, so the memo reads it as decided and does not ask for it again.

2 · Does it fit the funders?

Not yet confirmed.

BC Builds and the Community Housing Fund each need figures the option leaves blank: how many units rent below market, the rent tiers, the accessible units and the land tenure.

3 · What is still unsure?

Six open items, each with a named person.

The tree by-law asks for 20 trees on this 2,811 m² lot and the plan leaves 17, so the architect finds room for 3 more or the project pays $3,000 cash in lieu. The plate the setbacks leave holds 70 of the 105 homes. The development manager supplies the funders' figures.

4 · Does the money work?

Needs more sources. The gap is 70% of the capital cost.

A first-pass pro‑forma from dated, cited assumptions and the fund's provisional grant: a $37.8 million gap against a 15% feasibility bar, counting the 70 homes the first plans hold (no rent tiers are entered, so none of the fund's grant is counted), every line naming its source, the placeholders marked.

The words on this sheet

The building's floor area divided by the lot's area.
CD-1

Plate

03How it works

From an address and an option to a dated memo.

The rules sit in six layers, stored as data with a source and a date on every line. Ordinary code applies them, so the same inputs always give the same memo. A language model can draft a rule or a site fact, but a person confirms each one before the engine uses it.

  1. 01Site and option

    Give it an address, then shape the option.

    The address finds the parcel, the district and the lot in the City's open data, and the tool asks only what the data could not tell; "not sure" is a normal answer. Then it derives the option from the rules, funder-ready, outright or at the staff benchmark, with four figures to change in place.

  2. 02Rules

    Run the rules.

    The engine checks the option against 176 rules, each dated and cited; a CD-1 site is matched to its own by-law by number. A run takes under a second, and the same inputs under the same rules give the same memo.

  3. 03Memo

    Read the memo.

    The memo gives the four answers and names a person for every open item. The massing the rules allow is drawn in plan, section and 3D, with the plans by program and their exits checked against the building code, and a Revit sheet is built from the same numbers. The manager issues it to the architect with a note; the architect issues it back.

04Who it's for

One memo for three kinds of reader.

Today the rules for a site are read by different people at different stages, each from their own documents. The memo checks all of them on day one and puts the results where everyone reads them.

  1. 01Development manager

    Decides what goes to the board.

    The manager runs a site in three stops before any money goes to design, works through the open items, hands the run to the architect with a note and brings the PDF to the board meeting.

  2. 02Architect

    Tests options and draws them.

    The architect places the mass and chooses the front, lays out the plans block by block with the exits checked, runs it again, compares the two memos side by side, takes the massing into Revit and issues the run back to the manager.

  3. 03Board, planner, funder

    Reads the front sheet without a briefing.

    They get a dated, cited memo that opens on the ask and the four answers, and in which every finding shows its arithmetic and the person responsible for it.

05By the numbers

Small enough that a firm can maintain the rules by hand.

No AI decides an answer: the same inputs give the same memo. The engine is plain Python reading the rule files, and a language model only ever drafts a rule for a person to confirm.

  • 176

    rules in six layers, from the zoning district to the funders and the costs, each with its source and the date it took effect

  • 44

    zoning districts, nearly all read from the City's own PDFs, and two CD‑1 by-laws; a figure entered without its document says "unverified"

  • R1 R2 R3

    every memo on a site gets a revision number, like a drawing issue, so a rule change shows up as a new revision
